# Rules, penalties and stewards

## Who decides penalties
Incidents are judged by the stewards, a panel of officials including a former driver. They review video and data. The Race Director runs the session and may refer incidents to them. Fans often see the on-screen messages "noted", "under investigation", and then a decision.

## What "noted" and "under investigation" mean
"Incident noted" means the stewards have seen it and will look at it, but it has not been formally investigated yet. "Under investigation" means they are actively reviewing it and a penalty could follow. "No further investigation" or "no further action" means the incident is closed with no penalty. Decisions can take several laps, and some are made after the race.

## Time penalties (5 and 10 seconds)
A time penalty adds seconds to a driver's race time. The most common are 5 and 10 seconds. If the driver makes a pit stop, the penalty is served in the pit box: the car stops for the length of the penalty before mechanics can work on it. If the driver does not pit again, the time is added to their finishing time after the race. These are the usual outcome for causing a collision or similar infringement.

## Drive-through penalty
A drive-through is a penalty where the driver must go through the pit lane at the pit lane speed limit without stopping. It costs roughly 20 seconds or more, depending on the circuit. It is more serious than a 5 or 10 second time penalty.

## Stop-go penalty
A stop-go penalty means the driver must enter the pit lane, stop in their own pit box for a set time (usually 10 seconds) with no work allowed, and then rejoin. It is a heavier penalty than a drive-through.

## Serving penalties under Safety Car
Drive-through and stop-go penalties generally cannot be served while a Safety Car or Virtual Safety Car is out. The driver has to serve them once racing resumes.

## Grid penalty
A grid penalty moves a driver back some number of places on the starting grid. It is mostly given for technical reasons such as using more power unit components than allowed, or for a gearbox change, and sometimes for driving offences like causing a collision in an earlier race. If the penalty is larger than the number of cars ahead, the driver starts from the back.

## Pit lane start
A driver may be sent to start from the pit lane, behind the whole grid, for some breaches, such as changing a car's setup after qualifying outside the parc fermé rules. They leave the pits after the field passes.

## Penalty points on the super licence
Dangerous or reckless driving can earn penalty points on a driver's super licence. Points stay for 12 months, and a driver who reaches 12 points gets a one-race ban. Under the 2026 guidelines, penalty points are meant for dangerous or reckless incidents, deliberate collisions and unsportsmanlike behaviour, so stewards are likely to hand out fewer of them than before.

## Reprimand
A reprimand is the lightest sanction: an official warning with no time loss. It goes on the driver's record.

## Disqualification
A driver can be disqualified from a session or the whole event, usually after a technical check finds the car breaks the rules, for example it is under the minimum weight or a part is the wrong size. A black flag during the race is how a disqualification is shown on track.

## Causing a collision
If a driver is judged to have caused a collision, the stewards usually give a time penalty, often 5 or 10 seconds depending on severity, and sometimes super licence points. A racing incident with no one at fault gets no penalty.

## Track limits
Track limits are the white lines at the edge of the track. A driver must keep at least part of one tyre on or touching the white line. If all four wheels go fully beyond it, the driver has left the track. In qualifying, the lap time is usually deleted. In the race, drivers typically get warnings and then a penalty after repeated breaches. The 2026 guidelines let stewards delete a track limits breach if it came in a failed overtaking attempt with no clear advantage gained.

## Gaining a lasting advantage
If a driver goes off track while overtaking and ends up in front, they are expected to give the place back. If they do not, they can be penalised. The key question is whether the driver gained a lasting advantage by leaving the track.

## Defending and overtaking rules
A driver may defend their position but cannot make more than one move in reaction to an attacker, and must leave a car's width to the edge of the track. For an overtake, the attacker generally needs to be roughly alongside, with their front wheels about level with the other car's mirrors, by the apex to have a right to the corner. The 2026 guidelines say stewards look at the whole sequence of corners in a chicane or S-bend, not only a single corner.

## Unsafe release
An unsafe release is when a team lets a car leave its pit box into the path of another car, or without all wheels secure. The team is penalised, commonly with a time penalty of 5 or 10 seconds, and the car may have to stop.

## Pit lane speeding
The pit lane has a speed limit, usually 80 km/h, and lower at some tracks. Exceeding it brings a penalty, which in races is commonly a time penalty, plus a fine in some cases.

## Jump start
Sensors detect any car moving before the start lights go out. A jump start is usually punished with a 10 second penalty.

## Impeding in qualifying
In qualifying, a driver on a slow lap must not hinder another driver on a fast lap. If they do, they can get a penalty such as a grid drop or reprimand.

## Right of review
After a penalty, a team can ask the stewards to review the decision, but only if it brings significant new evidence that was not available at the time. It is not a simple appeal.

## Power unit component limits
Each driver can only use a limited number of each power unit element per season. If they exceed the allowance, they get grid place penalties. This is why you sometimes see drivers start from the back after "taking a new engine" or new parts, even when they did not do anything wrong on track.

## Parc fermé rules
Parc fermé means cars are placed under restricted conditions. From the start of qualifying until the race, teams cannot change the car's setup freely; only limited work such as repairs or tyre changes is allowed. On sprint weekends teams can change the setup between the Sprint and grand prix qualifying. A breach can lead to a pit lane start.

## Technical checks after sessions
After qualifying and the race, officials randomly check cars, including weight, floor and fuel. A failed check can mean disqualification. This is why results are provisional until all checks have been completed.
