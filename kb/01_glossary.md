# Glossary

## Apex
The apex is the innermost point of a corner, the spot the racing line clips. Hitting the apex well sets up a fast exit onto the next straight.

## Backmarker
A backmarker is a slower car running near the back of the field, often about to be lapped by the leaders. Leaders get blue flags to tell backmarkers to move aside.

## Blistering
Blistering is when a tyre overheats and bubbles form on its surface, which causes a sudden loss of grip. It is different from graining, which is caused by sliding rather than excess heat.

## Box, Box (pit call)
"Box, box" is the radio call telling a driver to come into the pits on this lap. "Box" just means the pit garage area. A call like "stay out" means the driver should not pit yet.

## Brake bias
Brake bias is how braking force is split between the front and rear wheels. Drivers adjust it from the steering wheel during the race as fuel burns off, tyres wear, and corners change.

## Chicane
A chicane is a quick sequence of two or three tight corners in alternating directions, usually put in to slow cars down before a fast section.

## Classified (in the results)
A driver is classified in the race result if they complete at least 90 percent of the winner's race distance, even if they did not finish on track because of a crash or failure. Unclassified cars appear below the finishers.

## Clean air
Clean air means a car has nothing directly ahead of it, so the air hitting its wings is smooth and the car performs at its best. The opposite is dirty air.

## Compound
A compound is the type of rubber mix in a tyre. Softer compounds give more grip but wear out faster, harder compounds last longer but are slower. See the tyres section for the colours.

## Constructors' Championship
The Constructors' Championship is the team title. Every point scored by a team's two drivers is added together, and the team with the most points at the end of the season wins it.

## Cost cap
The cost cap is a limit on how much money a team can spend on developing and running its car in a season. It exists to stop the richest teams from buying a permanent advantage. Some items, such as driver salaries, sit outside the cap. Breaking the cap is punished with fines and other sporting penalties.

## Degradation (deg)
Tyre degradation, usually just called deg, is the gradual loss of grip and lap time as a tyre wears out. Commentators say "high deg" when tyres are wearing quickly.

## Delta
Delta is a time difference between two laps or two cars. A negative delta means faster, positive means slower. Under a Virtual Safety Car, drivers must stay above a target delta shown on their steering wheel.

## DNF, DNS, DSQ
DNF means did not finish, DNS means did not start, and DSQ means disqualified. DSQ is usually a technical rule breach found in checks after the race, or a black flag during it.

## Downforce
Downforce is the aerodynamic force that presses the car into the track, so it can corner much faster. More downforce means more cornering grip but also more drag, which lowers straight-line speed.

## Drag
Drag is air resistance that slows the car, especially on straights. Teams balance downforce for the corners against low drag for the straights.

## DRS (Drag Reduction System, retired)
DRS was a flap on the rear wing that a chasing driver could open within one second of the car ahead to cut drag and get a speed boost. It ran from 2011 to 2025. It no longer exists in 2026: it has been replaced by active aerodynamics (Straight Mode and Corner Mode) and by Overtake Mode, which is a battery-power boost rather than a wing flap. Older articles, videos and games still mention DRS.

## Drivers' Championship
The Drivers' Championship is the individual title. The driver with the most points over the season wins it.

## Energy deployment and harvesting (2026)
In the 2026 cars, drivers and engineers constantly manage the battery. Deployment means using stored electrical energy to add power. Harvesting (or recharging) means putting energy back into the battery, for example under braking. A car with a lot of energy left can attack or defend with more power.

## ERS (Energy Recovery System)
ERS is the hybrid part of an F1 power unit. It recovers energy that would be wasted, stores it in a battery, and gives it back as extra electrical power to the car. In 2026 the electrical side of the power unit is much bigger than before.

## FIA
The FIA, the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile, is the governing body that writes and enforces the rules of Formula 1. Formula One Management (FOM) handles the commercial side, including TV rights.

## Fastest lap
The fastest lap is the quickest single lap of the race. Since 2025 it no longer earns a championship point, though it is still shown in purple on the timing screens and counted as a stat.

## Formation lap
The formation lap is the lap before the race start, when cars leave the pit lane, drive round to the grid, and warm up tyres and brakes by weaving and braking. No overtaking is allowed. The cars then take their grid slots for the start.

## Front row
The front row is the first two starting grid positions: pole position and second place, side by side. Starting on the front row is a big advantage because there is no one ahead for the first corner.

## Gap and interval
The gap is the time difference to the race leader, and the interval is the time difference to the car directly ahead. TV graphics show both next to each driver. In 2026 an interval below one second at the detection point matters for Overtake Mode.

## Graining
Graining is when small bits of rubber tear off the tyre surface because it is sliding, leaving a rough, grippy-looking but slower surface. It tends to fade away after some laps.

## Grid and grid slot
The grid is the starting formation, two cars per row, set by qualifying. A grid slot is the position a car starts from. Pole is slot one.

## Grid penalty
A grid penalty moves a car back a number of places from where it qualified, usually for changing too many power unit parts or a technical or driving breach. It is applied before the race starts.

## Halo
The halo is the curved titanium bar above the cockpit that protects the driver's head from large debris and impacts. It has been mandatory since 2018.

## Hairpin
A hairpin is a very tight corner, close to a 180-degree turn, taken at low speed. They are often good overtaking spots.

## In-lap and out-lap
An in-lap is the lap on which a driver drives into the pits. An out-lap is the lap on which they leave the pits. Teams often talk about being quick on the out-lap on fresh tyres, because that matters for the undercut.

## Kerb
A kerb (curb in the US) is the raised painted strip at the edge of the track at a corner. Drivers use it to take a faster line, but hitting the wrong one can unsettle or damage the car.

## Lap record
The lap record is the fastest lap ever set in a race at that circuit. A related term, track record, can include qualifying laps.

## Lapped car
A car is lapped when the race leader catches and passes it, putting it one full lap behind. Lapped cars are shown the blue flag so they let the leaders through.

## Lift and coast
Lift and coast means a driver lifts off the throttle before the braking zone and rolls into the corner to save fuel, protect tyres or brakes, or harvest battery energy. In 2026 it is a normal part of energy management, so you may hear engineers ask drivers to do it.

## Lights out
Lights out is the moment at the start when the five red lights above the grid go out and the race begins. Every car must stay still until then.

## Lock-up
A lock-up is when a driver brakes so hard that a tyre stops rotating and slides, producing a puff of smoke and often a flat spot on the tyre that causes vibrations.

## Manage (as in "manage the pace")
When an engineer tells a driver to manage, it means to not push flat out and to protect tyres, brakes, fuel or battery energy, usually because the gap to the next car is comfortable. The opposite is "push".

## Marbles
Marbles are small pieces of rubber thrown off tyres that collect off the racing line. Driving through them is very slippery, which is why overtaking wide can be risky.

## Marshals
Marshals are the volunteers around the circuit who wave flags, clear debris and help with recoveries. Many are trained and are real heroes of the sport.

## MGU-K and MGU-H
The MGU-K (Motor Generator Unit, Kinetic) recovers energy under braking and delivers extra power to the wheels. The MGU-H (Heat) recovered energy from exhaust heat via the turbo; it has been removed in 2026, which made the power unit simpler, and in 2026 the MGU-K is far more powerful.

## Monocoque
The monocoque, or tub, is the strong carbon-fibre survival cell that the driver sits in. The engine, suspension and wings attach to it. It is crash-tested heavily.

## Oversteer and understeer
Oversteer is when the rear of the car slides out and the car turns more than the driver intended. Understeer is when the front tyres lose grip and the car pushes wide, turning less than intended.

## Overcut and undercut
These are pit stop strategies. An undercut means pitting earlier than the car ahead, using fresh tyres to set fast laps, and coming out in front after they stop. An overcut means staying out longer on older tyres, hoping to be quick enough in clean air to get ahead when the rival pits.

## Paddock
The paddock is the area behind the pit garages where teams have their motorhomes, hospitality and offices. Journalists, drivers and guests gather there.

## Parc fermé
Parc fermé (French for closed park) means the cars are under restricted conditions and the teams cannot freely work on them. Once qualifying begins, teams can only make tightly limited changes, so the setup they choose is close to what they race. See the rules section for details.

## Pit lane, pit box and pit wall
The pit lane is the road next to the track where cars stop for service. Each team has a pit box, its own marked bay where mechanics work. The pit wall is the row of control desks where engineers and strategists watch data and run the race.

## Pit stop
A pit stop is when a car enters the pit lane so the crew can change tyres and make minor adjustments. The wheel change itself takes roughly two seconds for a good crew, and most of the time lost is from driving the pit lane at a limited speed. Refuelling has been banned since 2010.

## Pole position
Pole position is first place on the starting grid, given to the driver who sets the fastest lap in qualifying. In the sprint format there are two poles: one for the sprint and one for the grand prix.

## Podium
The podium is the platform where the top three finishers receive trophies. A driver finishing in the top three is said to be "on the podium".

## Power unit
The power unit is what F1 calls its engine system. It is a 1.6 litre V6 turbo combustion engine plus the hybrid electrical parts. The 2026 version is a big overhaul described in the 2026 cars section.

## Purple sector
On the timing screens a sector time in purple is the fastest anyone has gone in that sector so far in the session. Green is a driver's own personal best, and yellow is slower than their best.

## Push
Push means drive at maximum pace. "Push, push, push" on the radio usually means the team sees an opportunity, such as getting an undercut, or needs the driver to build a gap.

## Race Director
The Race Director runs the event from race control: starts races, decides on Safety Cars and red flags, and manages the safety of the track. The Race Director is different from the stewards, who judge incidents and give penalties.

## Reprimand
A reprimand is a formal warning from the stewards for a minor breach. It carries no time penalty, but a driver who collects repeated reprimands can face further sanctions.

## Retirement
A retirement means the driver stopped racing, either because of a crash, mechanical failure, or by team decision. It shows as DNF if they did not complete enough laps to be classified.

## Sector and mini-sector
Every lap is split into three sectors, each timed separately. Many circuits also have much smaller mini-sectors that power the live colour coded timing bars.

## Slipstream (tow)
Slipstream, also called the tow, is the reduced air resistance a car gets when driving close behind another one on a straight. It lets the following car go faster than it could alone, helping with overtaking.

## Stewards
The stewards are the officials who investigate incidents and decide on penalties. They watch replays and data and can reach a decision during or after the race. You will see messages like "under investigation" on screen.

## Stint
A stint is the period a driver spends on track between two pit stops, on one set of tyres.

## Super licence
A super licence is the permit every F1 driver needs. Penalty points for driving offences are added to it. Reaching 12 points within 12 months gives a one-race ban.

## Team orders
Team orders are instructions from a team to its drivers, such as letting a teammate through or holding position. They are allowed in Formula 1, though some are unpopular with fans.

## Team principal
The team principal is the boss of the team, who leads the pit wall and speaks for the team to the media.

## Traction
Traction is the grip that lets the car put its power down when accelerating, especially out of slow corners.

## Tyre cliff
A tyre cliff is when a worn tyre suddenly loses lots of grip instead of fading slowly, causing lap times to fall away sharply. Teams try to pit before the cliff.

## Tyre warmers
Tyre warmers are electric blankets teams wrap around tyres before they are fitted so they work well straight away. They are used in the garage and on the grid, but not during the race.

## Weaving on the formation lap
Drivers swerve left and right on the formation lap to put heat into their tyres. It is not a sign that something is wrong.

## Aquaplaning
Aquaplaning is when a tyre rides on a thin film of standing water instead of gripping the track, so the driver loses steering and braking for a moment. It is a big danger in heavy rain, and it is one reason races get stopped or start behind the Safety Car.

## Banker lap
A banker lap is a safe, solid lap set early in a qualifying session so a driver is sure to progress to the next round, before taking more risks on later attempts.

## Common abbreviations (FP1, Q1, SQ, GP, SC, VSC)
FP1, FP2 and FP3 are the three free practice sessions. Q1, Q2 and Q3 are the qualifying rounds. SQ1 to SQ3 are the Sprint Qualifying rounds. GP is grand prix, SC is Safety Car, VSC is Virtual Safety Car, and P1, P2 and so on mean first position, second position and so on.

## Cool-down lap
The cool-down lap is the slow lap after the chequered flag, when drivers return to the pits to park in parc fermé. Cars are often slowed to let the engine and brakes cool, and drivers sometimes wave to fans or pick up flags.

## Differential
The differential lets the two driven rear wheels turn at different speeds in a corner. F1 drivers adjust its settings from the steering wheel to change how the car turns in and puts power down.

## Dive-bomb
A dive-bomb is an aggressive overtake where a driver brakes very late and lunges into a gap from far back. If the car is not properly alongside by the apex, it can end in contact and a penalty.

## Flat spot
A flat spot is a worn patch on a tyre caused by a lock-up. It makes the car vibrate, and drivers often ask for a stop to change the tyre.

## Flying lap (hot lap, push lap)
A flying lap is a full-speed timed lap, such as in qualifying. A driver gets onto one by first doing a slower preparation lap to warm the tyres and make space.

## Gearbox
F1 cars use a semi-automatic gearbox with eight forward gears, changed by paddles on the steering wheel, and shifts take only a few thousandths of a second. Teams can only use a limited number of gearboxes before facing a penalty.

## Green track
A green track is one with little rubber on the surface, such as at the start of a weekend or after rain, so grip is low and gets better as more cars run.

## Ground effect
Ground effect is how the shaped underside of the car, the floor, creates downforce by sucking the car towards the track. It was brought back in 2022 and is a main source of downforce on modern F1 cars.

## Hat-trick and grand slam
A hat-trick is when a driver takes pole position, wins the race, and sets the fastest lap in the same weekend. A grand slam is a hat-trick where they also lead every lap of the race.

## Homologation
Homologation is the official approval of a part, such as a power unit design or a safety structure, as legal and safe. Once approved, changes are tightly restricted.

## Installation lap
An installation lap is a short outing to check that the car is working properly, such as before qualifying or the race. The driver returns to the pits straight after without setting a competitive time.

## Lights-to-flag win
A lights-to-flag win is when a driver leads from the start lights until the chequered flag without ever losing the lead on track.

## Pit limiter
The pit limiter is a button that holds the car to the pit lane speed limit. Drivers press it as they enter the pit lane and release it at the exit. Speeding in the pit lane is penalised.

## Pit window
The pit window is the range of laps in which a pit stop makes sense, based on tyre wear and the gaps to other cars. Teams talk about "opening the window" when a stop becomes worthwhile.

## Porpoising
Porpoising is when a car bounces up and down at high speed because the airflow under the floor stalls and recovers repeatedly. It was a big problem when ground effect returned in 2022, and teams had to change setups to reduce it.

## Race sim and long run
A race sim or long run is when a team uses practice to run many laps on race-type tyres and fuel, to learn the pace and tyre wear over a full stint. These runs tell you more about race pace than a single fast lap.

## Racing line
The racing line is the fastest path round the track, usually wide on entry, close to the apex, and wide on exit. Drivers leave it to defend or pass, but the rubber on that line gives the most grip.

## Reserve driver
A reserve driver is a team's stand-in who can take over if a race driver is ill or injured. They often work in the simulator and help the team in the garage.

## Scrutineering
Scrutineering is the FIA's technical checking of cars, such as weight and dimensions, to make sure they follow the rules. A failed check can mean disqualification.

## Skid block (plank)
The skid block, or plank, is a wooden strip under the car's floor, used to stop teams from running the car too low. If it is worn down more than allowed after the race, the car can be disqualified.

## Telemetry
Telemetry is the live data stream from many sensors on the car, such as speeds, temperatures and tyre pressures, sent to the team's engineers so they can monitor and adjust the car during the session.

## Track evolution (rubbering in)
Track evolution is the way a track gets faster as a session goes on, because cars lay down rubber and clear dust. It is why drivers usually go quicker at the end of a qualifying session.

## Train (of cars)
A train is a line of cars bunched up behind a slower car, usually because overtaking is difficult. Drivers in the middle of a train often suffer from dirty air and worn tyres.

## Wheelspin
Wheelspin is when the driven wheels spin faster than the car's speed on acceleration, which wastes power and wears tyres. Drivers manage it with careful throttle control, especially out of slow corners.

## Wind tunnel and CFD
The wind tunnel and CFD, computational fluid dynamics, are the tools teams use to design and test the aerodynamics of the car. F1 rules limit how much each team may use them, to keep the playing field fairer.
