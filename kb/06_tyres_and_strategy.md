# Tyres and strategy

## Who supplies the tyres
Pirelli is the sole tyre supplier for Formula 1. All teams use the same tyres, so strategy comes from how and when each team uses them. The tyres are 18 inch.

## Dry tyre colours
For dry weather, Pirelli has a range of slick compounds, from the hardest to the softest. For each race weekend it picks three of them and names them Hard, Medium and Soft. The sidewall marking shows the colours: white for Hard, yellow for Medium and red for Soft. On the TV graphics next to each driver you will see a coloured tyre icon with the number of laps it has done.

## Soft, medium and hard: what is the difference
Soft tyres give the most grip and the fastest lap time, but wear out the quickest. Hard tyres last longest but are slower, especially when new. Medium sits in between. Many races are decided by when a team chooses to switch between them.

## Wet weather tyres
There are two wet tyres. Intermediates, marked with green, are for a damp or drying track. Full wets, marked with blue, are for heavy rain, and they can pump away large amounts of water. When it is raining, you often see drivers swap between intermediates and slicks as the track dries, and timing the change is a big strategic call.

## Tyre sets and allocation
Every driver gets a limited number of new tyre sets for the weekend, spread over soft, medium and hard. Teams must plan which sets to use in practice, qualifying and the race.

## What is a pit stop strategy
A strategy is the plan for when to pit and which tyres to use. A one-stop strategy means pitting once, a two-stop means pitting twice. Teams choose based on tyre wear, track position and the risk of a Safety Car, and change the plan during the race as things develop.

## One-stop versus two-stop
A one-stop saves time in the pits and avoids traffic, but puts more wear on each tyre. A two-stop costs about 20 extra seconds in the pits but allows the driver to push harder on fresher tyres. Which is faster depends on how fast the tyres degrade at that circuit.

## Track position
Track position means where a car runs on the road. At circuits where overtaking is hard, being ahead on track is worth more than having better tyres, because a faster car can get stuck in dirty air behind a slower one.

## Dirty air and following another car
When a car follows closely, it drives in the turbulent air the car in front leaves behind. This cuts downforce and can overheat the tyres and brakes. It makes it harder to follow through corners and is a main reason overtaking is so hard. The 2026 cars have been designed to reduce this effect.

## The undercut in practice
Pitting before a rival gives a driver fresh, grippy tyres, which can allow a quick out-lap while the rival is still on old tyres. When the rival pits the next lap, the undercutter can come out ahead. The undercut works best at tracks where tyres wear fast and where there is open track to rejoin into.

## The overcut in practice
The overcut works when staying out longer is faster, for example when the track is clear and fresh tyres take time to warm up. The driver who stays out stays in clean air, sets quick laps, and then pits and rejoins ahead.

## Offset strategy
An offset strategy is when two drivers, or two rivals, are on different tyre choices or pit at different moments, so they are on different tyre ages. Later in the race, the driver on fresher tyres may be able to catch and pass those on older ones.

## Tyre management
Tyre management means driving smoothly, avoiding wheelspin and lock-ups, and sometimes going slightly slower so the tyres last longer. When you hear an engineer say "manage the tyres" or "look after the rears", this is what they mean.

## Why cars do not all pit at once
Teams pit at different laps to avoid queues, to react to rivals, and to take advantage of gaps in traffic. A pit stop at the wrong time can drop a driver behind slower cars, which loses more time than the stop itself.

## Safety Car strategy gamble
If a Safety Car comes out, teams that have not yet pitted can swap tyres cheaply, so strategists watch for chances. Sometimes a team will gamble by staying out on old tyres to keep track position.
