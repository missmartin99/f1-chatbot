# Race control: Safety Car, VSC, red flags

## Safety Car (SC)
The Safety Car is a real car that comes out onto the track after a serious incident, such as a crash or debris, to slow the whole field down while marshals work. All cars must line up behind it with a limited speed, and no overtaking is allowed. The gaps between cars are closed up, which can erase a big lead. Pit lanes stay open, so teams often pit then.

## Why teams love a Safety Car pit stop
When the field is slowed under the Safety Car, a driver who pits loses much less time than during normal racing. This is sometimes called a "cheap" or "free" pit stop. It can transform strategy, so drivers who just pitted can be unlucky and those who pitted just after are lucky.

## Safety Car restart
When the track is clear, the Safety Car comes into the pits at the end of a lap, its lights go out, and the race restarts. The leader controls the pace until the control line, then drivers can race flat out and overtake. Restarts often cause action because tyres are cold on the slow lap, and cars are bunched together.

## Lapped cars and the Safety Car
When the Safety Car is out and the lights go green, lapped cars may overtake the cars between them and the leader, so they can rejoin at the back of the queue, which removes the lap they had lost. This is called unlapping.

## Virtual Safety Car (VSC)
The Virtual Safety Car is for smaller incidents, like a stopped car near the track, when a full Safety Car is not needed. There is no physical car. Every driver must slow down by roughly a third and stay above a target time shown on the steering wheel, called a delta. Overtaking is not allowed, and the gaps between cars stay roughly the same, so the race order is less disturbed than with a Safety Car.

## SC and VSC: how they differ
A Safety Car bunches the field up behind a pace car and wipes out gaps. A Virtual Safety Car slows everyone down in place and keeps the gaps. The VSC is shown on TV as a "VSC" banner, and the Safety Car as "SC".

## Red flag stoppage
A red flag stops the session or race completely. Cars go slowly back to the pit lane and queue at the pit exit. During a red flag teams may repair the car and change tyres, since no time is being lost on track. The race resumes with a restart, usually a standing start, after the track is cleared. The clock for the maximum race time continues up to its limit.

## Why there are red flags
Red flags happen after heavy crashes, when a car or debris is blocking the track, when barriers need repair, or when heavy rain makes conditions too dangerous. Red flags in qualifying freeze the session clock.

## Race suspension and delayed start
If rain or safety concerns delay a start, the race may begin behind the Safety Car. A race can also begin that way in heavy rain, with a standing or rolling restart later when the track is safer.

## Race Director and race control
Race control is a room where officials run the event, using cameras, marshal reports and data. The Race Director decides when to deploy a Safety Car, VSC or red flag, and controls the start.

## Pit stops and pit lane basics
In a pit stop, the car stops in its team's box and about twenty mechanics change all four wheels in a couple of seconds. Cars must follow the pit lane speed limit, and the team must release the driver safely. A normal stop costs a driver around 20 to 25 seconds in total from entering to leaving the pit lane, mostly in driving the pit lane at the limit.

## Double stack
A double stack is when a team pits both of its drivers on consecutive laps, so the second car has to wait in the queue behind the first while the crew finishes. It can cost the second driver time, but teams use it under Safety Car conditions to save time overall.

## Why cars cannot refuel
Refuelling during a race has been banned since 2010 for safety and cost reasons. Cars start with all the fuel they need for the race, so they get lighter as the race goes on, and lap times usually improve if tyres are good.
